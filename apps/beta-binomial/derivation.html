THE MATH BENEATH THE GARDEN

Why Beta is conjugate.

A Beta prior and a binomial likelihood produce a Beta posterior. Here is how the algebra makes that happen, one step at a time.

The setupThe updateThe normalizerWorked example
01 / SET THE SCENE

One unknown success probability.

Suppose we observe n independent binary trials, each with the same unknown success probability \theta. Let Y be the number of successes and y its observed value. We treat the number of trials as fixed.

Y\mid\theta,n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,\theta),\qquad \theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)

The parameters satisfy \alpha,\beta>0, 0<\theta<1, and y\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}. The prior describes uncertainty about the success probability before these observations.

02 / WRITE THE TWO INGREDIENTS

A density for the prior. A likelihood for the count.

The normalized Beta prior density is

p(\theta)=\frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\,\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}.

The beta function supplies the normalizing constant:

\begin{aligned}B(a,b)&=\int_0^1 t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt\\[4pt]&=\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)},\qquad a,b>0.\end{aligned}

The binomial likelihood is

p(y\mid\theta,n)=\binom{n}{y}\theta^y(1-\theta)^{n-y}.

Once the data are observed, we view the likelihood as a function of \theta. It is not itself a probability density over \theta. The binomial coefficient counts the possible trial sequences with exactly y successes.

03 / APPLY BAYES’ RULE

Multiply the likelihood by the prior.

p(\theta\mid y,n)=\frac{p(y\mid\theta,n)\,p(\theta)}{p(y\mid n)}.

The denominator does not depend on \theta. To identify the posterior’s shape, we can first work up to proportionality:

\begin{aligned}p(\theta\mid y,n)&\propto p(y\mid\theta,n)\,p(\theta)\\[6pt]&=\frac{\binom{n}{y}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\,\theta^y(1-\theta)^{n-y}\,\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}.\end{aligned}

For fixed data and prior parameters, both \binom{n}{y} and B(\alpha,\beta) are constants with respect to \theta. They affect normalization, but not the shape of the posterior as a function of \theta.

04 / COLLECT THE POWERS

Successes add to α. Failures add to β.

\begin{aligned}p(\theta\mid y,n)&\propto\theta^{y+\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{n-y+\beta-1}\\[6pt]&=\theta^{(\alpha+y)-1}(1-\theta)^{(\beta+n-y)-1}.\end{aligned}

This has the same functional form as a Beta density. Only its two shape parameters have changed:

\alpha'=\alpha+y,\qquad\beta'=\beta+n-y.

That is conjugacy: the prior and posterior belong to the same distribution family. A Beta prior is conjugate to a binomial likelihood. Conjugacy describes the algebra; it does not by itself tell us which prior parameters to choose.

05 / PUT THE CONSTANT BACK

Make the posterior integrate to one.

Integrating the likelihood times the prior gives the marginal probability of the observed count (also called the evidence):

\begin{aligned}p(y\mid n)&=\int_0^1p(y\mid\theta,n)\,p(\theta)\,d\theta\\[6pt]&=\frac{\binom{n}{y}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\int_0^1\theta^{\alpha+y-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+n-y-1}\,d\theta\\[6pt]&=\binom{n}{y}\frac{B(\alpha+y,\beta+n-y)}{B(\alpha,\beta)}.\end{aligned}

Here and throughout, dependence on the fixed prior parameters is implicit in the notation. Substitute this expression into Bayes’ rule. The binomial coefficient and the original beta-function factor cancel:

p(\theta\mid y,n)=\frac{\theta^{\alpha+y-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+n-y-1}}{B(\alpha+y,\beta+n-y)}.
\boxed{\theta\mid y,n\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+y,\beta+n-y)}

This is a proper density because both updated shape parameters are positive. With no observations, n=y=0, so the posterior is exactly the prior.

06 / THE EXAMPLE IN YOUR R SCRIPT

Ten trials. Seven successes. A small update.

The demo starts with a Beta(10, 10) prior. The observed sequence is (1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1): seven successes and three failures.

\begin{aligned}\alpha'&=10+7=17,\\\beta'&=10+(10-7)=13.\end{aligned}
\theta\mid y=7,n=10\sim\operatorname{Beta}(17,13).

The prior mean is 0.5 and the observed success fraction is 0.7. The posterior mean lies between them:

\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid y,n]=\frac{\alpha+y}{\alpha+\beta+n}=\frac{17}{30}\approx0.5667.

For any nonzero number of trials, we can rewrite that mean as a weighted average:

\begin{aligned}\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid y,n]&=\underbrace{\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+n}}_{\text{prior weight}}\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\\[5pt]&\quad+\underbrace{\frac{n}{\alpha+\beta+n}}_{\text{data weight}}\frac{y}{n}.\end{aligned}
\frac{20}{30}\times0.5+\frac{10}{30}\times0.7=\frac{17}{30}.

The prior concentration \alpha+\beta sets the prior’s weight in this formula. It need not count real past trials. Adding observations increases the data’s weight.

ONE MORE CONNECTION

Where the “beta-binomial” distribution comes in.

The posterior for \theta is Beta. The beta-binomial distribution describes a count after we average a binomial model over a Beta-distributed success probability. The evidence above is its prior predictive form.

For K successes in m future trials, use the updated parameters instead:

\begin{aligned}p(K=k\mid y,n)&=\int_0^1p(k\mid\theta,m)\,p(\theta\mid y,n)\,d\theta\\[6pt]&=\binom{m}{k}\frac{B(\alpha'+k,\beta'+m-k)}{B(\alpha',\beta')},\\[4pt]&\hspace{30pt}k=0,1,\ldots,m.\end{aligned}

This is the posterior predictive distribution shown in the app. It accounts for uncertainty in the success probability as well as variation in the future trials.
